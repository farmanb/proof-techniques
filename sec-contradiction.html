    Section 3 Proof by Contradiction

    Suppose that we want to prove some proposition \(P\) (which might be something like \(A \implies B\) or even more complicated). One approach, called proof by contradiction, is to assume \(\neg P\) and then logically deduce a contradiction of the form \(Q \wedge \neg Q\text{,}\) where \(Q\) is some proposition. Since this is absurd, the assumption \(\neg P\) must have been false, so \(P\) is true. The tricky part about a proof by contradiction is that it is not usually obvious what the statement \(Q\) should be.
    🔗

    Proof of \(P\) by Contradiction.

    Here is what the general structure for a proof by contradiction looks like if we are trying to prove the proposition \(P\text{.}\)
    🔗
    [State any upfront assumptions.] For sake of a contradiction, assume \(\neg P\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \(\ldots\) [Use definitions and known results to derive some \(Q\) and its negation \(\neg Q\text{.}\)] \(\ldots\)
    🔗
    This is a contradiction. Therefore, \(P\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.1.

    There is no largest even integer.
    🔗
    Solution.
    For sake of a contradiction, assume that there is a largest even integer, say \(m\text{.}\) Since \(m\) is even, there is an integer \(k\) such that \(m = 2k\text{,}\) and so
    🔗
    \begin{gather*} m + 2 = 2k + 2 = 2(k+1) \end{gather*}
    🔗
    is also even. Moreover, \(m + 2 > m\text{.}\) This contradicts the assumption that \(m\) is the largest even integer. Therefore, there is no largest even integer.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Proof by contradiction can be useful for proving statements of the form \(A \implies B\text{,}\) where \(\neg B\) is easier to “get your hands on,” because \(\neg(A \implies B)\) is logically equivalent to \(A \wedge \neg B\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Proof of \(A \implies B\) by Contradiction.

    If you want to prove the implication \(A \implies B\) via a proof by contradiction, then the structure of the proof is as follows.
    🔗
    [State any upfront assumptions.] For sake of a contradiction, assume \(A\) and \(\neg B\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \(\ldots\) [Use definitions and known results to derive some \(Q\) and its negation \(\neg Q\text{.}\)] \(\ldots\)
    🔗
    This is a contradiction. Therefore, if \(A\text{,}\) then \(B\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.2.

    Let \(x\) be a rational number and let \(y\) be an irrational number. Then \(x + y\) is irrational.
    🔗
    Solution.
    For sake of a contradiction, assume \(x\) is rational, \(y\) is irrational, and \(x + y\) is rational. Since the difference of two rational numbers is rational,
    🔗
    \begin{gather*} y = (x+y) - x \end{gather*}
    🔗
    is rational. This contradicts the assumption that \(y\) is irrational. Therefore, if \(x\) is rational and \(y\) is irrational, then \(x+y\) is irrational.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 3.3.

    A direct proof is usually considered more elegant than a proof by contradiction, and you should generally prefer one when it is available. A telltale sign that a proof by contradiction was unnecessary is when, after assuming \(A\) and \(\neg B\text{,}\) you derive \(\neg B\) and \(B\) themselves as your contradiction—that argument can usually be rewritten as a direct proof of \(A \implies B\text{.}\) On the other hand, if the contradiction you reach involves some \(Q\) genuinely different from \(B\text{,}\) a proof by contradiction was likely the right tool.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.4 An Unnecessary Proof by Contradiction.

    If \(n\) is an even integer, then \(n^2\) is even.
    🔗
    Solution.
    For sake of a contradiction, assume \(n\) is even and \(n^2\) is odd. By definition, there is an integer \(k\) such that \(n = 2k\text{.}\) Then
    🔗
    \begin{gather*} n^2 = (2k)^2 = 4k^2 = 2(2k^2), \end{gather*}
    🔗
    so \(n^2\) is even. This contradicts the assumption that \(n^2\) is odd. Therefore, if \(n\) is even, then \(n^2\) is even.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Notice that the assumption \(\neg B\) (that \(n^2\) is odd) was never actually used—the computation \(n^2 = 2(2k^2)\) shows \(n^2\) is even using only \(A\) (that \(n\) is even). The contradiction we reached is exactly \(B\) and \(\neg B\text{,}\) which is the telltale sign from Note 3.3 that a direct proof was available all along. To transform this into a direct proof, delete the “for sake of a contradiction” framing and the assumption \(\neg B\text{,}\) and simply conclude with the fact that was derived:
    🔗
    Proof. Assume \(n\) is even. By definition, there is an integer \(k\) such that \(n = 2k\text{.}\) Then
    🔗
    \begin{gather*} n^2 = (2k)^2 = 4k^2 = 2(2k^2), \end{gather*}
    🔗
    so \(n^2\) is even.
    🔗
    This is precisely the direct proof given in Example 1.1.
    🔗
