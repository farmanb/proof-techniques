    Section 6 Proof by Induction

    Mathematical induction is a proof technique for statements of the form \((\forall n \in \N)P(n)\text{,}\) or more generally \((\forall n \in \Z)(n \geq a \implies P(n))\text{,}\) where \(P(n)\) is some predicate and \(a \in \Z\text{.}\) The Principle of Mathematical Induction says that if \(P(1)\) is true, and for all \(k \geq 1\text{,}\) \(P(k)\) implies \(P(k+1)\text{,}\) then \(P(n)\) is true for all \(n \in \N\text{.}\) Verifying the first hypothesis is called the base step (or base case), and verifying the second is called the inductive step. Note that the inductive step is itself a proof of a quantified conditional, so inside it you are filling in Proof of \((\forall x)(A(x) \implies B(x))\).
    🔗

    Proof of \((\forall n \in \N)P(n)\) by Induction.

    Here is the general structure for a proof by induction.
    🔗
    [State any upfront assumptions.] We proceed by induction.
    🔗
    1. Base step: [Verify that \(P(1)\) is true. This often, but not always, amounts to plugging \(n = 1\) into two sides of some claimed equation and verifying that both sides are actually equal.]
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Inductive step: [Your goal is to prove “For all \(k \in \N\text{,}\) if \(P(k)\) is true, then \(P(k+1)\) is true.”] Let \(k \in \N\) and assume that \(P(k)\) is true. [Do something to derive that \(P(k+1)\) is true.] Therefore, \(P(k+1)\) is true.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Thus, by induction, \(P(n)\) is true for all \(n \in \N\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 6.1.

    For all \(n \in \N\text{,}\) \(3\) divides \(4^n - 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    We proceed by induction.
    🔗
    1. Base step: When \(n = 1\text{,}\) \(4^1 - 1 = 3\text{,}\) which is divisible by \(3\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Inductive step: Let \(k \in \N\) and assume that \(3\) divides \(4^k - 1\text{.}\) By definition, there is an integer \(m\) such that \(4^k - 1 = 3m\text{.}\) Then
      🔗
      \begin{align*} 4^{k+1} - 1 \amp= 4 \cdot 4^k - 1\\ \amp= 4(4^k - 1) + 3\\ \amp= 4(3m) + 3\\ \amp= 3(4m + 1). \end{align*}
      🔗
      Since \(4m + 1 \in \Z\text{,}\) it follows that \(3\) divides \(4^{k+1} - 1\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Thus, by induction, \(3\) divides \(4^n - 1\) for all \(n \in \N\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    The base case need not be \(n = 1\text{.}\) To prove that \(P(n)\) holds for all integers \(n \geq a\text{,}\) verify \(P(a)\) in the base step and take \(k \geq a\) in the inductive step.
    🔗

    Proof of \((\forall n \in \Z)(n \geq a \implies P(n))\) by Induction.

    Here is the general structure for a proof by induction when the base case does not necessarily involve \(a = 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    [State any upfront assumptions.] We proceed by induction.
    🔗
    1. Base step: [Verify that \(P(a)\) is true.]
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Inductive step: [Your goal is to prove “For all \(k \geq a\text{,}\) if \(P(k)\) is true, then \(P(k+1)\) is true.”] Let \(k \geq a\) be an integer and assume that \(P(k)\) is true. [Do something to derive that \(P(k+1)\) is true.] Therefore, \(P(k+1)\) is true.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Thus, by induction, \(P(n)\) is true for all integers \(n \geq a\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 6.2.

    For all integers \(n \geq 4\text{,}\) \(2^n \lt n!\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    We proceed by induction.
    🔗
    1. Base step: When \(n = 4\text{,}\) \(2^4 = 16 \lt 24 = 4!\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Inductive step: Let \(k \geq 4\) be an integer and assume that \(2^k \lt k!\text{.}\) Then
      🔗
      \begin{gather*} 2^{k+1} = 2 \cdot 2^k \lt 2 \cdot k! \leq (k+1) \cdot k! = (k+1)!, \end{gather*}
      🔗
      where the last inequality uses \(2 \leq k + 1\text{,}\) which holds since \(k \geq 4\text{.}\) Therefore, \(2^{k+1} \lt (k+1)!\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Thus, by induction, \(2^n \lt n!\) for all integers \(n \geq 4\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    There is another formulation of induction, called complete induction (or strong induction), in which the inductive step assumes not just \(P(k)\) but all of \(P(1), P(2), \ldots, P(k)\text{.}\) Complete induction is not more powerful than ordinary induction, but it is the right tool when reaching \(P(k+1)\) requires more than the single preceding case—for instance, when \(P(k+1)\) depends on both \(P(k)\) and \(P(k-1)\text{.}\) In such situations, you will typically need to verify more than one case in the base step; the number of base cases depends on how far the inductive step “looks back.”
    🔗

    Proof of \((\forall n \in \N)P(n)\) by Complete Induction.

    Here is the general structure for a proof by complete induction.
    🔗
    [State any upfront assumptions.] We proceed by induction.
    🔗
    1. Base step: [Verify that \(P(1)\) is true. Depending on the statement, you may also need to verify that \(P(k)\) is true for other specific values of \(k\text{.}\)]
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Inductive step: [Your goal is to prove “For all \(k \in \N\text{,}\) if \(P(j)\) is true for all \(j \in \N\) such that \(j \leq k\text{,}\) then \(P(k+1)\) is true.”] Let \(k \in \N\text{.}\) Suppose \(P(j)\) is true for all \(j \leq k\text{.}\) [Do something to derive that \(P(k+1)\) is true.] Therefore, \(P(k+1)\) is true.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Thus, by complete induction, \(P(n)\) is true for all \(n \in \N\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 6.3.

    Define a sequence by \(a_1 = 1\text{,}\) \(a_2 = 3\text{,}\) and \(a_n = 3a_{n-1} - 2a_{n-2}\) for all \(n \geq 3\text{.}\) Then \(a_n = 2^n - 1\) for all \(n \in \N\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    We proceed by induction. Since the recurrence for \(a_{k+1}\) involves both \(a_k\) and \(a_{k-1}\text{,}\) we need two base cases.
    🔗
    1. Base step: \(a_1 = 1 = 2^1 - 1\) and \(a_2 = 3 = 2^2 - 1\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Inductive step: Let \(k \geq 2\) and suppose \(a_j = 2^j - 1\) for all \(j \leq k\text{.}\) Then, since \(k + 1 \geq 3\text{,}\) the recurrence gives
      🔗
      \begin{align*} a_{k+1} \amp= 3a_k - 2a_{k-1}\\ \amp= 3(2^k - 1) - 2(2^{k-1} - 1)\\ \amp= 3 \cdot 2^k - 3 - 2^k + 2\\ \amp= 2 \cdot 2^k - 1\\ \amp= 2^{k+1} - 1. \end{align*}
      🔗
      Therefore, \(a_{k+1} = 2^{k+1} - 1\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Thus, by complete induction, \(a_n = 2^n - 1\) for all \(n \in \N\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 6.4.

    In Example 6.3, the inductive step starts at \(k \geq 2\) rather than \(k \geq 1\text{,}\) because the recurrence only applies once there are two previous terms. The case \(k = 1\) (that is, \(P(2)\)) is covered by the base step instead. Forgetting to check \(a_2\) separately is the most common mistake in this kind of argument.
