    Section 5 Quantified Statements

    At this point, we should be able to use our understanding of quantification to construct counterexamples to complicated false propositions and proofs of complicated true propositions. Here are some general proof structures for various logical forms. Assume \(U\) is the universe of discourse.
    🔗

    Direct Proof of \((\forall x)P(x)\).

    Here is the general structure for a direct proof of the proposition \((\forall x)P(x)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    [State any upfront assumptions.] Let \(x \in U\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \(\ldots\) [Use definitions and known results.] \(\ldots\)
    🔗
    Therefore, \(P(x)\) is true. Since \(x\) was arbitrary, for all \(x\text{,}\) \(P(x)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 5.1.

    For every integer \(n\text{,}\) \(n^2 - n\) is even.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Let \(n \in \Z\text{.}\) Note that \(n^2 - n = n(n-1)\) is a product of two consecutive integers, so one of \(n\) and \(n - 1\) is even; write that even factor as \(2k\) for some \(k \in \Z\text{.}\) Then \(n(n-1) = 2k \cdot (\text{the other factor})\text{,}\) which is twice an integer. Therefore, \(n^2 - n\) is even. Since \(n\) was arbitrary, for all \(n \in \Z\text{,}\) \(n^2 - n\) is even.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Combining Direct Proof of \((\forall x)P(x)\) with Proof of \(A \implies B\) by Direct Proof, we obtain the following skeleton proof.
    🔗

    Proof of \((\forall x)(A(x) \implies B(x))\).

    Below is the general structure for a direct proof of the proposition \((\forall x)(A(x) \implies B(x))\text{.}\)
    🔗
    [State any upfront assumptions.] Let \(x \in U\text{.}\) Assume \(A(x)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \(\ldots\) [Use definitions and known results to derive \(B(x)\)] \(\ldots\)
    🔗
    Therefore, \(B(x)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 5.2.

    For every integer \(n\text{,}\) if \(6\) divides \(n\text{,}\) then \(3\) divides \(n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Let \(n \in \Z\text{.}\) Assume \(6\) divides \(n\text{.}\) By definition, there is an integer \(k\) such that \(n = 6k\text{.}\) Then
    🔗
    \begin{gather*} n = 6k = 3(2k), \end{gather*}
    🔗
    and since \(2k \in \Z\text{,}\) it follows that \(3\) divides \(n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof of \((\forall x)P(x)\) by Contradiction.

    Here is the general structure for a proof of the proposition \((\forall x)P(x)\) via contradiction.
    🔗
    [State any upfront assumptions.] For sake of a contradiction, assume that there exists \(x \in U\) such that \(\neg P(x)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \(\ldots\) [Do something to derive a contradiction.] \(\ldots\)
    🔗
    This is a contradiction. Therefore, for all \(x\text{,}\) \(P(x)\) is true.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 5.3.

    For every integer \(n\text{,}\) \(n^2 \neq 2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    For sake of a contradiction, assume that there exists \(n \in \Z\) such that \(n^2 = 2\text{.}\) Since \(1^2 = 1 < 2 < 4 = 2^2\text{,}\) we have \(1 < \abs{n} < 2\text{.}\) But there is no integer strictly between \(1\) and \(2\text{.}\) This is a contradiction. Therefore, for all \(n \in \Z\text{,}\) \(n^2 \neq 2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Direct Proof of \((\exists x)P(x)\).

    Here is the general structure for a direct proof of the proposition \((\exists x)P(x)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    [State any upfront assumptions.] \(\ldots\)
    🔗
    \(\ldots\) [Use definitions, axioms, and previous results to deduce that an \(x\) exists for which \(P(x)\) is true; or if you have an \(x\) that works, just verify that it does.] \(\ldots\)
    🔗
    Therefore, there exists \(x \in U\) such that \(P(x)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 5.4.

    There exists an even prime number.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The number \(2\) is prime, and \(2 = 2 \cdot 1\) is even. Therefore, there exists an even prime number.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof of \((\exists x)P(x)\) by Contradiction.

    Below is the general structure for a proof of the proposition \((\exists x)P(x)\) via contradiction.
    🔗
    [State any upfront assumptions.] For sake of a contradiction, assume that for all \(x \in U\text{,}\) \(\neg P(x)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \(\ldots\) [Do something to derive a contradiction.] \(\ldots\)
    🔗
    This is a contradiction. Therefore, there exists \(x \in U\) such that \(P(x)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 5.5.

    Among any three consecutive integers \(n, n+1, n+2\text{,}\) at least one is divisible by \(3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    For sake of a contradiction, assume that none of \(n\text{,}\) \(n+1\text{,}\) \(n+2\) is divisible by \(3\text{.}\) Every integer leaves a remainder of \(0\text{,}\) \(1\text{,}\) or \(2\) upon division by \(3\text{;}\) since \(n\) is not divisible by \(3\text{,}\) either \(n\) leaves remainder \(1\) or remainder \(2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    • If \(n\) leaves remainder \(1\text{,}\) then \(n + 2\) leaves remainder \(0\text{,}\) so \(3\) divides \(n+2\text{.}\)
      🔗
    • If \(n\) leaves remainder \(2\text{,}\) then \(n + 1\) leaves remainder \(0\text{,}\) so \(3\) divides \(n+1\text{.}\)
      🔗
    🔗
    Either way, one of \(n\text{,}\) \(n+1\text{,}\) \(n+2\) is divisible by \(3\text{,}\) contradicting our assumption. Therefore, at least one of \(n, n+1, n+2\) is divisible by \(3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 5.6.

    A counterexample to a false statement \((\forall x)Q(x)\) is exactly a proof of \((\exists x)(\neg Q(x))\text{,}\) so it can be produced by following Direct Proof of \((\exists x)P(x)\). Also, several of these skeleton proofs are sometimes combined in a single proof: for instance, to prove \((\forall x)(P(x) \implies Q(x))\) by contradiction, you would begin by assuming that there exists \(x \in U\) such that \(P(x)\) and \(\neg Q(x)\text{.}\)
